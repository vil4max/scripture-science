# Research: Timeline and Scope of the Religious Picture of the World

Research for the religion-map project. Every fact below carries the URL of a page opened in this session, a verbatim excerpt (original language, trimmed with "..." where needed to stay near 25 words), and a source tier:

- **[official]** — a tradition's own body, text, or self-maintained reference, used for its own dates and self-identification.
- **[reference]** — Pew Research Center, Encyclopaedia Britannica, an academic/university encyclopedia (e.g. *Encyclopedia of Religion*, *New Catholic Encyclopedia*, Gale reference works, EBSCO Research Starters), or a comparable scholarly source.
- **[news/NGO]** — reputable journalism or a nonprofit resource, used only where no official or reference source could be opened.

**Access note:** Encyclopaedia Britannica (britannica.com) returned HTTP 403 (Cloudflare bot-challenge) to both WebFetch and curl for every article tried in this session, so it could not be used directly despite being the project's preferred neutral source. Wikipedia and other wikis/aggregators were used only for discovery; where a fact came from one, the page actually cited and opened is given instead, or the item is marked `TODO: verify`.

**Scope and method note:** in line with the project's editorial rules, this document uses neutral, non-evaluative wording throughout (no "cult," "heresy," "sect," or similar) and does not rank traditions by truth-value. Where a tradition's own dating differs from a scholarly consensus (Section 3 vs. Section 4), both are presented side by side without privileging either. Numbers and dates below are attributed to the specific publication that stated them, not asserted as objective fact, since several — especially adherent counts for small traditions and pre-modern calendrical epochs — vary by source and methodology.

## 1. Which traditions count as monotheistic

Pew's 2025 global report groups most of the traditions below into a single residual bucket: "The 'other religions' category includes Baha'is, Daoists, Jains, Shintoists, Sikhs, Wiccans, Zoroastrians and many small groups, some of which can be described as folk or traditional religions" [reference]. Source: Pew Research Center, *How the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020* (PDF, p. 2), https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/06/PR_2025.06.09_global-religious-change_report.pdf — Pew does not break monotheistic out from non-monotheistic within that category, so classification below relies on tradition-specific reference sources.

### Zoroastrianism
- Classification: "Zoroastrianism is the monotheistic faith established by the Persian prophet Zoroaster" and holds "there is one supreme deity, Ahura Mazda (Lord of Wisdom)" [reference]. World History Encyclopedia, https://www.worldhistory.org/Zoroastrianism/
- Scholarly caveat: the same article notes Ahura Mazda's "eternal opponent, Angra Mainyu, is all-evil" — the coexistence of a strong evil counter-principle is the basis of the academic debate over whether the religion is better described as monotheistic, dualistic, or henotheistic (debate identified via search synthesis; a direct academic-journal quote on the Hintze/Kellens position could not be opened — Cambridge Core returned 403 and SOAS's repository returned 403). `TODO: verify` a direct quote from the academic monotheism-vs-dualism debate.
- Dating: "Zarathushtra probably lived and preached in Central Asia between the eighteenth and fifteenth centuries bce," while traditional dating places him at "588 bce" [reference]. *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/zoroastrianism
- Adherents: "the faith has a following of approximately 300,000 persons worldwide" [reference], same source/URL (figure dated to the article's compilation; current estimates in circulation range higher — `TODO: verify` a more recent count).

### Sikhism
- Founding: "Sikhi was founded in 1469 by Guru Nanak, who was born a Hindu in the Punjab region" [news/NGO]. Religion Media Centre factsheet, https://religionmediacentre.org.uk/factsheets/sikh-tradition-factsheet/
- Classification: "Sikhi is a broadly monotheistic faith which believes in one, timeless and invisible God" [news/NGO], same source.
- Adherents: "There are more than 25 million Sikhs around the world, making Sikhi the fifth-largest religion in the world" [news/NGO], same source.

### Bahá'í Faith
- Classification (self-description): "God, the Creator of the universe, is all-knowing, all-loving and all-merciful" [official]. Official Bahá'í site, https://www.bahai.org/beliefs/god-his-creation/
- Adherents and founding date: not confirmed by an opened source in this session (bahai.org's statistics page returned 404; the front page and beliefs pages give no figures). `TODO: verify` worldwide adherent count and founding date (Baháʼu'lláh, 19th-century Persia) from an official Bahá'í statistics page or a reference source.

### Druze
- Founding: "The Druze religious doctrine was founded, approved, supervised, or simply tolerated by the sixth Fāṭimid caliph, al-Manṣūr, known as al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh (r. 996–1021 ce)" [reference]. *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/druze
- Classification: "The Druze believe that God is One and All is God" [reference]. *Gale Encyclopedia of Multicultural America* via Encyclopedia.com, same URL.
- Adherents: "There are approximately one million Druzes in the world today" [reference], same URL. Note: the faith is closed to conversion in either direction, which distinguishes it from actively proselytizing monotheisms; this specific closed-conversion detail was found via search synthesis and needs a direct-quote check (`TODO: verify`).

### Samaritans
- Relationship to Judaism: "The Samaritan religion is an outgrowth of the Israelite-Jewish religion as it existed around the beginning of the common era" and "the basic monotheistic outlook of Samaritanism is the same as in Judaism" [reference]. Reinhard Pummer, "Samaritans," *Encyclopedia of Religion* (2005) via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/samaritans-0
- Origin as distinct group: "It is now virtually common opinion among scholars of Samaritanism that the origin of the Samaritans as a distinct religion has to be sought in Maccabean times" [reference], same source.
- Adherents: "In the early twenty-first century the community comprises approximately 660 members" [reference], same source (2005 figure). `TODO: verify` a current count — Wikipedia (discovery only, not citable directly) suggests growth to roughly 900 by 2024, but no primary/reference source for that figure was opened in this session.

### Mandaeans
- Classification: "Sabian-Mandaeans constitute an ethno-religious group of less than 100,000 people who practise a monotheistic religion" [news/NGO]. Fanack Foundation, https://fanack.com/religions-in-the-middle-east-and-north-africa/mandaeism/
- Origin claim: the same article attributes to "French anthropologist Claire Lefort" the view that Mandaean "first traces...may date back to Sumerian times, more than 3,000 years ago" [news/NGO], same URL. This is markedly earlier than the mainstream scholarly placement of Mandaean origins in the early centuries CE in Mesopotamia; only one source for the Sumerian-era claim was found. `TODO: verify` against an academic source (e.g. Encyclopaedia Iranica's Mandaeans entry, which returned 403 in this session).
- Current population: "no more than 300 families remaining" in Iraq, with most having emigrated [news/NGO], same URL.

### Rastafari
- Classification: "Rastafarians believe in the existence of one supernatural spirit, whom they call Jah and associate with Haile Selassie" [reference]. *International Encyclopedia of the Social Sciences* (Gale) via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/rastafarianism
- Founding: "Rastafari began in Jamaica in the 1930s, but most scholarship recognizes its connection to earlier Jamaican folk religions, such as Revival" [reference], same source.
- Adherents: only a Jamaica-specific figure was confirmed — "estimates put adherents in Jamaica at between seventy thousand and a hundred thousand, or 3 percent to 4 percent of the population" [reference] (*Encyclopedia of World Cultures* via Encyclopedia.com, same URL). `TODO: verify` a global figure (unconfirmed search snippets suggest 700,000–1,000,000 worldwide, not independently opened).

### Recommendation for the map

Include as core "other monotheistic traditions," ranked by strength of self-identification, scholarly classification, and notable numbers:
1. **Sikhism** (~25–30 million) and **Bahá'í** (Abrahamic self-understanding, global spread) — least ambiguous cases; unambiguous monotheistic self-identification confirmed above.
2. **Zoroastrianism** — historically important as the world's oldest continuously practiced monotheistic-claiming faith and frequently discussed alongside Judaism/Christianity/Islam in comparative religion; include with an explicit note on the monotheism/dualism scholarly debate.
3. **Druze** and **Samaritans** — smaller (~1 million and ~660–900 respectively) but both are well-documented, self-identify as strictly monotheistic, and are directly relevant to the Abrahamic lineage tree (Druze emerging from Ismaili Islam, Samaritans as a parallel branch of Israelite religion).
4. **Mandaeans** — smallest and most obscure (under 100,000, most sources agree), but self-identifies as monotheistic and is notable for its Gnostic features and veneration of John the Baptist; include with a caveat on classification.
5. **Rastafari** — include with the most caveats: monotheistic belief in Jah is clear, but the movement's Haile Selassie christology and lack of a single confirmed global population figure make it a harder fit for a "monotheistic traditions" comparison than the others; recommend a shorter, clearly-caveated entry rather than a full comparison-table row.

## 2. Pagan and other non-monotheistic religions (timeline briefs)

Each entry below is a candidate one-line timeline card: a scholarly date or range, a short neutral description, and the source. Ordered roughly by earliest attested date.

### Ancient Egyptian religion
- Date: "At the end of the predynastic period (c.3200 BC), when a combined state was created, a national religion apparently grew out of the various primitive tribal and local religions" [reference]. Before that, "probably the oldest form of religious worship in Egypt was animal worship."
- Description: state-level polytheism unifying local and tribal animal-cults after Egypt's political unification.
- Source: *The Columbia Encyclopedia*, 6th ed., via Encyclopedia.com, http://www.encyclopedia.com/reference/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/egyptian-religion

### Atenism (Akhenaten's reform)
- Date: Akhenaten reigned "c. 1352–1336 bce," i.e. roughly 1350 BCE as in the project brief [reference].
- Description: the pharaoh's short-lived cult of the sun-disc Aten, described in this source as "the founder of the earliest historically documented monotheistic religion" — flagged here as a pagan-timeline entry because it did not outlast Akhenaten's reign and Egyptian polytheism resumed afterward.
- Source: *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/akhenaton

### Mesopotamian (Sumerian/Akkadian/Babylonian)
- Date: the belief that humans exist to labor for the gods "was already firmly in place by the time of the Uruk period (circa 4100-3100 BCE)" [reference].
- Description: polytheistic city-state religion in which humans served as co-laborers with the gods to maintain cosmic order and hold back chaos.
- Source: World History Encyclopedia, https://www.worldhistory.org/Mesopotamian_Religion/

### Canaanite
- Date: "Firsthand evidence for Canaanite culture in the second millennium bce (or, in archaeological terms, the Middle Bronze and Late Bronze periods) comes from artifactual evidence found at many archaeological sites" [reference].
- Description: Levantine Bronze Age polytheism centered on the deities El, Baal, and Anat, known chiefly through Ugaritic texts.
- Source: *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/canaanite-religion-overview

### Greek
- Date: the earliest datable cult site in this source's own timeline is "c. 1500 BCE First temple to Demeter & Persephone is built at Eleusis" [reference]; the pantheon later coheres around Mount Olympus under Homeric influence.
- Description: "In the ancient Greek world, religion was personal, direct, and present in all areas of life" [reference] — a civic and household polytheism rather than a centrally organized church.
- Source: World History Encyclopedia, https://www.worldhistory.org/Greek_Religion/

### Roman
- Date: "The history of Roman religion before the 5th century b.c. is obscure, and it is likewise complex, because Roman religion from an early stage exhibits a fusion of Italic and Etruscan elements with the Roman core proper" [reference].
- Description: state polytheism that progressively absorbed Etruscan, Italic, and later Greek deities and cults, lasting until Christianity's ascendancy in the 4th century CE.
- Source: *New Catholic Encyclopedia* via Encyclopedia.com, http://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/roman-religion

### Germanic/Norse
- Date: origins are prehistoric and undated in the sources found; what is dated is the end point — conversion "took more than 700 years, from the 4th century in the South (Gothi) to the end of the 10th century in the North (Scandinavia), to displace the pagan Germanic religion by Christianity" [reference].
- Description: polytheistic religion of the Germanic-speaking peoples, known mainly through Roman-era accounts and later Old Norse literary sources (the Eddas).
- Source: *New Catholic Encyclopedia* via Encyclopedia.com, http://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/germanic-religion

### Slavic
- Date: "The term Slavic religion can be used to refer to the mythology and cultic life common to all Slavs from the sixth to the tenth century" [reference].
- Description: a polytheistic and animistic system, "dominated by male warrior gods but preserving ancient feminine deities rooted in agricultural fertility traditions," that receded as Slavic peoples Christianized.
- Source: *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/slavic-religion

### Celtic
- Date: "Greek and Roman writers supply valuable information on Celtic religion from the 3d century b.c." [reference] — the religion itself is older, but this is the earliest period for which written outside description survives.
- Description: polytheistic Iron Age religion of Western Europe, associated with the druid priesthood and known largely through classical and later medieval sources.
- Source: *New Catholic Encyclopedia* via Encyclopedia.com, http://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/celtic-religion

### Hinduism (Vedic period)
- Date: Indo-Aryan migration into India "in about 1500 bce"; the Vedic literature itself "evolved into its present form over nine or ten centuries between about 1400 and 400 bce" [reference].
- Description: the polytheistic sacrificial religion of the Rigveda and the other Vedas, historically foundational for later Hinduism (the project brief's "Vedic period" scope).
- Source: *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/hinduism

### Jainism
- Date: "FOUNDED: c. 550 b.c.e." in one reference source's summary; a fuller academic treatment notes traditional Mahavira dates (599–527 bce) are revised by scholars "to about 490 to 410 bce," aligned with revised Buddha dating [reference].
- Description: ascetic South Asian tradition following the teachings of a lineage of Jina ("conqueror") teachers, of whom Mahavira is the most recent.
- Source: Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/jainism

### Buddhism
- Date: "FOUNDED: Fifth century b.c.e."; the Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama) was born "in the middle of the sixth century b.c.e." [reference].
- Description: the teaching of Siddhartha Gautama on liberation from suffering through the Eightfold Path, first preached at Sarnath.
- Source: *New Catholic Encyclopedia* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/buddhism

### Confucianism
- Date: "FOUNDED: c. 1050–256 b.c.e." (the broader Zhou-dynasty range in which the tradition's sources sit); its central figure, "Master Kong," lived "551–479 b.c.e." [reference].
- Description: an ethical and social tradition, "a 'diffused' one that permeated existing social entities, such as the family and the state," rather than a separate priesthood.
- Source: Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/confucianism

### Taoism
- Date: "Daoism (also called Taoism) developed in China, perhaps as early as the sixth century bce," traditionally attributed to "Laozi (born c. 604 bce)" [reference].
- Description: a Chinese tradition centered on the Dao, "the rhythmic balance and natural, flowing patterns of the universe."
- Source: Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/taoism

### Shinto
- Date: kami veneration is ancient but its antiquity as a distinct "religion" is scholarly contested; the formal, government-organized system is dated concretely to "the years following 1868" (the Meiji Restoration), when "a nationwide system of shrines, priests, and doctrines relating only to non-Buddhist divinities...was developed with government support" [reference].
- Description: indigenous Japanese veneration of kami (spirits), later separated administratively from Buddhism and formalized as a state institution.
- Source: *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/shinto

### Indigenous/traditional religions (one line)
- Not a historical date but a live count: "An estimated 405 million people – or about 6% of the world's total population – are adherents of folk or traditional religions" [reference].
- Description: "faiths that are closely associated with a particular group of people, ethnicity or tribe. They often have no formal creeds or sacred texts" [reference] — covers African traditional religions, Chinese folk religion, Native American religions, and similar traditions as one grouped timeline line.
- Source: Pew Research Center, https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2012/12/18/global-religious-landscape-folk/

## 3. Traditional creation and calendar epochs

Each tradition below counts years from its own reckoning of a foundational event. Dates are given as each source states them (proleptic Julian unless noted).

### Byzantine (Orthodox) era — 1 September 5509 BC
- Excerpt: "Its year one, the supposed date of creation, was September 1, 5509 BC to August 31, 5508 BC," a computation "derived from the Septuagint version of the Bible." It was "the official era of the Eastern Orthodox Church from ca. AD 691 to 1728" in the Ecumenical Patriarchate, used by the Byzantine Empire "from AD 988 to 1453, and by Russia from c. AD 988 to 1700."
- Source: OrthodoxWiki (a community-maintained reference associated with the Orthodox Church in America), https://orthodoxwiki.org/Byzantine_Creation_Era
- Tier: [official]-adjacent — a community-maintained Orthodox reference wiki, not an official church document or an academic chronology. `TODO: verify` against a primary patristic or academic source (Britannica's own `anno-mundi` article, the natural [reference]-tier source, returned 403 in this session).

### Alexandrian era — 25 March 5493 BC
- Excerpt: "The Alexandrian computation of the date of creation was worked out to be 25 March 5493 BC." The system originated with the monk Panodoros, whom "Bishop Annianos of Alexandria...shifted...by about six months, to begin on 25 March" in AD 412.
- Source: same as above, https://orthodoxwiki.org/Byzantine_Creation_Era
- Tier: [official]-adjacent, same caveat as the Byzantine era above.

### Jewish anno mundi — 7 October 3761 BCE
- Excerpt: "October 7, 3761 B.C.E., is the date on which the world was created – according to the Hebrew calendar that governs the passage of time among the Jewish people," a figure reached because "Rabbi Yose ben Halafta, who lived in the 2nd century CE...[b]asing himself on no source but the bible...sat down and did the math."
- Source: Haaretz, https://www.haaretz.com/jewish/2015-10-07/ty-article/3761-bce-the-world-is-created/0000017f-e4be-d7b2-a77f-e7bf482b0000
- Tier: [news/NGO] — Britannica's `anno-mundi` article, the more natural [reference]-tier source for this fact, returned 403 in this session. `TODO: verify` against it or another reference source.

### Ussher chronology — 23 October 4004 BC
- Excerpt: confirmed independently by four reference works found on the same page: *Encyclopedia of Irish History and Culture* — Ussher "dated the creation of the world to 23 October 4004 b.c.e."; *New Catholic Encyclopedia* — "Ussher put the creation of the world at 4004 b.c."; *The Oxford Companion to British History* — "his argument that the world was created in 4004 bc held the field for decades"; *The Concise Oxford Dictionary of World Religions* — "the world was created in 4004 BCE."
- Source: all four via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/protestant-christianity-biographies/james-ussher
- Tier: [reference] — the best-sourced epoch in this section, four independent academic reference works agreeing.

### Jehovah's Witnesses (Adam's creation) — 4026 BCE
- Excerpt: "Adam created: 4026 B.C.E." — the organization's own timeline entry.
- Source: Watchtower Online Library, https://wol.jw.org/en/wol/d/r1/lp-e/1102013279
- Tier: [official] — the tradition's own publication, matching the project's source-order rule for a tradition's own dates.

### Hijri epoch — 622 CE (16 July 622, Julian)
- Excerpt: "The Islamic Calendar, also known as the Hijri or lunar calendar, began on July 16, 622 CE, marking the first day of the first year of the Hegira (Hijra)."
- Source: EBSCO Research Starters, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/ethnic-and-cultural-studies/start-islamic-calendar/
- Tier: [reference].

### Buddhist era — 543/544 BCE
- Excerpt: "The Buddhist Era began with the Parinibbāna or Passing away of the Buddha in the year 543 BCE," with the working formula "the Buddhist Year for 2021 is B = 544 + 2021 = B.E. 2565" reflecting the one-year offset between national traditions (Thai/Lao/Khmer count from 543 BCE, Burmese/Sri Lankan from 544 BCE).
- Source: Wesak.org.my, a Malaysian Buddhist community organization's own explainer, https://wesak.org.my/calculating-b-e/
- Tier: [official]-adjacent — a Theravada Buddhist community's own reckoning of its own calendar, matching the project's source-order rule for a tradition's own dates.

### Kali Yuga — 3102 BCE (18 February, proleptic Julian)
- Excerpt: traced only as far as Wikipedia's citation chain, not independently opened. The ultimate primary source is Aryabhata's *Aryabhatiya* (499 CE), quoted there as: "When the three yugas (satyug, tretayug and dwaparyug) have elapsed and 60 x 60 (3,600) years of kaliyug have already passed, I am now 23 years old" — from which 3102 BCE is derived arithmetically (3600 − (476 + 23) + 1). The academic secondary source named is Matchett & Yano's chapter in *The Blackwell Companion to Hinduism* (ed. Gavin Flood, 2003).
- Source: none independently opened — attempts via Encyclopedia.com (`aryabhata-i`) returned 500 errors, and no WebSearch budget remained to locate an open-access copy of the *Aryabhatiya* translation or the Blackwell chapter.
- Tier: not independently verified. `TODO: verify` by opening the *Aryabhatiya* or the Matchett & Yano chapter directly.

## 4. Traditional vs. scholarly dating: Abraham, Moses/Exodus, David

### Abraham
- Traditional/biblical chronology: "BORN: c. 2050 bce • Ur, Mesopotamia DIED: c. 1950 bce • Hebron, Canaan" [reference]. *Encyclopedia of Religion* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/abraham
- Scholarly view: "No external records have been found as yet that refer by name to Abraham or to any personage directly connected with him," and mid-20th-century attempts "to marshal...evidence from archeological discoveries at Nuzi, Mari, and elsewhere to provide a historical setting for Abraham in the second millennium b.c.e. have not withstood the test of time"; contemporary scholarship "tends to see Abraham as a fictitious symbolic model of faith" [reference]. *Encyclopaedia Judaica* via Encyclopedia.com, same URL.

### Moses / the Exodus
- Traditional/range dating found in reference works: "The events have been variously dated by scholars between about 1580 and 1200 bc" [reference]. *The Oxford Pocket Dictionary of Current English* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/exodus
- Scholarly consensus on historicity: "Current scholarly consensus based on archaeology holds the enslavement and exodus traditions to be unhistorical" [reference]. *Encyclopaedia Judaica* via Encyclopedia.com, same URL.
- Note: the commonly cited "early date" (1446 BCE, from 1 Kings 6:1's 480-year figure) vs. "late date" (13th century BCE, linked to Ramesses II/the Merneptah Stele) framing is well attested in the wider literature but was not independently confirmed by an opened source in this session (Encyclopedia.com's Merneptah Stele page returned 500 errors). `TODO: verify`.

### David
- Traditional dating: "King of ancient Israel (c.1010–970 bc), successor of Saul," death "c.962 bc" [reference]. *World Encyclopedia* via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/david
- Scholarly/archaeological debate ("Low Chronology"): "a small group of biblical scholars, known as 'the revisionists,' started to question" the standard chronology; proponents "suggested that major findings previously dated to the 10th century BCE should be redated to the 9th century," concluding "there was no archaeological evidence consistent with the United Monarchy." But "most scholars have not accepted the Low Chronology," and radiocarbon recalibration points many back toward "around 980-970 BCE" [news/NGO]. The Times of Israel, https://www.timesofisrael.com/despite-academic-battle-royal-a-new-book-returns-davids-kingdom-to-its-place-in-history/
- Note: the Tel Dan Stele ("bytdwd"/"House of David" inscription, discovered 1993) is widely cited as the key epigraphic evidence for David's historicity, but a direct quote from an opened source specifically discussing it could not be obtained in this session. `TODO: verify`.

## 5. Global adherent numbers

### Pew Research Center, *How the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020* (released 9 June 2025)

Source: https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/06/PR_2025.06.09_global-religious-change_report.pdf (PDF, pp. 8–11) and https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/how-the-global-religious-landscape-changed-from-2010-to-2020/ — [reference]

Methodology, verbatim: "This analysis is based on more than 2,700 sources of data, including national censuses, large-scale demographic surveys, general population surveys and population registers," covering "201 countries and territories that had populations of at least 100,000 people in 2010 or 2020," collectively "99.98% of the world's population." Data on country totals come from "the 2024 revision of the United Nations' World Population Prospects." The report was produced with the Center for Advanced Studies of Population and Religion (CASPAR) at Kraków University of Economics.

| Group | 2010 (% of world pop.) | 2020 (% of world pop.) | 2020 (number) | Change since 2010 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Christians | 30.6% | 28.8% | 2.3 billion | +121.6 million |
| Muslims | 23.9% | 25.6% | ~2.0 billion | +346.8 million |
| Religiously unaffiliated | 23.3% | 24.2% | 1.9 billion | +270.1 million |
| Hindus | 15.0% | 14.9% | 1.2 billion | +126.3 million |
| Buddhists | 4.9% | 4.1% | 324 million | −18.6 million |
| Other religions | 2.2% | 2.2% | ~0.2 billion | +18.1 million |
| Jews | 0.2% | 0.2% | 14.8 million | +0.9 million |

Verbatim highlights, by group (all from the same PDF, pp. 8–11):
- Christians: "remained the world's biggest religious group," but "the number of Christians rose by 122 million, reaching 2.3 billion" while "as a share of the world's population, Christians fell 1.8 percentage points, to 28.8%."
- Muslims: "the fastest-growing religious group over the decade" — "the number of Muslims increased by 347 million – more than all other religions combined," and "the share of the world's population that is Muslim rose by 1.8 points, to 25.6%."
- Religiously unaffiliated: "the number of religiously unaffiliated people rose by 270 million, reaching 1.9 billion," and "the share of 'nones' climbed nearly a full percentage point, to 24.2%" — the only category besides Muslims to grow as a share of world population.
- Hindus: "the number of Hindus rose by 126 million, reaching 1.2 billion," while "as a proportion of the global population, Hindus held steady at 14.9%."
- Buddhists: "the only major religious group that had fewer people in 2020 than a decade earlier" — "the number of Buddhists worldwide dropped by 19 million, declining to 324 million," slipping "0.8 points, to 4.1%" of world population.
- Jews: "the number of Jews worldwide grew by nearly 1 million, reaching 14.8 million," remaining "the smallest group in the study, representing about 0.2% of the world's population."
- All other religions combined (Baha'is, Daoists, Jains, Sikhs, folk religions, and other small groups): "expanded in tandem with the rest of the world. Their share of the global population held steady at 2.2%."
- Overall: "Collectively, 75.8% of the world's people identified with a religion as of 2020," down from 76.7% in 2010, "while the share without an affiliation has risen by the same amount."
- On why unaffiliation still grew despite an older, lower-fertility population: "unaffiliated people continued to grow as a share of the global population because many affiliated people around the world – primarily Christians – are 'switching' out of religion."
